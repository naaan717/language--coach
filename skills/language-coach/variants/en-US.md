## Coach voice

Questions, model sentences, spelling, and the pronunciation you coach all stay American for this session. British spellings and word choices in the learner's own speech are not mistakes unless strict consistency is on.

## Writing system

Prefer the American spelling. The second form is the usual British one. It is valid English. Leave it alone unless the learner asked for a single American set.

| American spelling to prefer | Usual British spelling, not an error by itself |
| --- | --- |
| color, honor, favor, labor | colour, honour, favour, labour |
| organize, realize, recognize | organise, realise, recognise |
| center, theater, liter | centre, theatre, litre |
| defense, offense | defence, offence |
| traveled, canceled, modeling | travelled, cancelled, modelling |
| gray, tire, plow, check (bank) | grey, tyre, plough, cheque |
| story (of a building), airplane | storey, aeroplane |
| jewelry, aging | jewellery, ageing |
| math, toward | maths, towards |

Details a coach needs:

- The *-ize* / *-ise* split is the Greek-derived suffix only. Do not rewrite *surprise*, *advise*, *exercise*, *comprise*, *supervise*, or *compromise*.
- *practice* and *license* cover both noun and verb. *program* covers software and a TV or event program.
- *judgment*, *catalog*, and *dialog* are all ordinary. *dialogue* is still widely used and is fine.
- The unit and the measuring device are both *meter*. *aluminum* is the American spelling (and has its own pronunciation below).

## Everyday expressions

These are current spoken American English. Gloss them in the explanation language when you teach one, and keep the example in English.

- **You bet.** Glad acceptance. "Can you send it over?" "You bet."
- **Hang out.** Spend unstructured time together. "We should hang out this weekend."
- **My bad.** A light apology for a small slip. "My bad — I had the wrong time."
- **No big deal.** It does not matter. "Missing the intro was no big deal."
- **Piece of cake.** Easily done. "The second take was a piece of cake."
- **On the fence.** Undecided. "I'm still on the fence about the offer."
- **Ballpark.** A rough figure or range. "Can you give me a ballpark?"
- **Touch base.** Make brief contact. "I'll touch base with you Thursday."
- **Call it a day.** Stop work. "Let's call it a day."
- **I'll take it from here.** I can continue without more help.

Skip dated slang. One expression in a turn is enough.

## Grammar and usage

These are preferences of General American, not universal laws. The British pattern on the right is still correct English.

- **Collective nouns** take a singular verb when the group is one unit. "The team is ready." "The committee has decided." A plural verb is not wrong when the members are clearly in view; do not correct it unless the learner asked for a consistent American pattern.
- **Simple past for recent events** is ordinary. "I just ate." "Did you see it yet?" Present perfect ("I've just eaten", "Have you seen it yet?") is also correct. Do not "fix" it into the simple past.
- **Possession** is usually *have* / *do you have*. "I have a car." "Do you have a minute?" *I've got* / *have you got* is understood and not an error.
- **Weekend.** Say *on the weekend* or *over the weekend*. *At the weekend* is British; mention it only under strict consistency.
- **different than** is ordinary American usage. *different from* is also fine. Do not correct either.
- **gotten** is the normal past participle when the meaning is change or acquisition. "She's gotten better." "He's gotten three offers." *Got* for possession ("I've got time") needs no correction.
- **Articles and places.** Say *in the hospital* even when someone is a patient. *In hospital* is British.
- **Ranges.** *Monday through Friday*. *Write me* is ordinary; *write to me* is also fine.
- **Past forms to prefer, without condemning the other:** *burned*, *dreamed*, *spelled*, *learned*. *burnt*, *dreamt*, *spelt*, *learnt* are acceptable.
- Do not teach regional patterns such as *needs washed* as the model.

## Pronunciation

Aim at General American. Other American accents are not errors. Do not drill a feature the learner's region does not use unless they asked for this model specifically.

**Rhotic /r/.** Pronounce /r/ wherever it is spelled in *car*, *hard*, *bird*, *teacher*, *course*. There is no dropped *r* and no linking *r* to add.

**TRAP and BATH.** Both sets use /æ/. *bath*, *laugh*, *dance*, *can't*, *ask*, *path*, *grass*, *chance* match *cat* and *trap*, not the vowel of *father*.

**Flap.** When /t/ or /d/ sits between vowels and the following syllable is unstressed, it becomes a quick flap [ɾ]. Drill this in *water*, *city*, *better*, *party*, *little*. *latter* and *ladder* may sound the same. Do not flap /t/ at the start of a stressed syllable (*attack*, *return*). While this file is active, do not replace the flap with a crisp British /t/ in the model voice. If the learner produces a crisp /t/, let it pass unless strict consistency is on; it is clear and understandable.

**LOT and THOUGHT.** *lot*, *cot*, *top*, *clock* use an unrounded /ɑ/, close to *father*. Many General American speakers also merge *cot* and *caught*. A learner who keeps them apart is not wrong. Do not force the merger, and do not import the rounded British /ɒ/.

## Consistency

Stay American in everything you produce. If the learner says *flat*, *lift*, *at the weekend*, or spells *colour*, understand them and move on. Offer the American form when the two words would send a listener to the wrong meaning (*pants*, *chips*, *football*, *first floor*), when they ask which one to use, or when strict consistency is on. In that last case, call it the American choice, not a correction of bad English.
